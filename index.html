Demand and inventory planning simulation
SYNTHETIC DATA

A forecasting, 30-week supply plan and exception-review pipeline for a simulated consumer-electronics business, plus three planning decisions I tested by re-running the plan.

Full report →

Question

A supply team commits orders weeks before demand shows up, and an overall fill rate can hide a node that is failing badly. How do you turn uncertain demand into a supply plan a planner can act on, and which decisions would actually change that plan? All of the data is synthetic: 10 consumer-electronics SKUs across 3 channels and 2 U.S. regions (60 demand series, 104 weeks of history). No real company data was used, and the table design is my own.

Approach

Stage 1 forecasts customer orders, not sell-through, because sell-through is capped by stockouts and a model trained on it under-forecasts exactly where availability failed. I held out the last 13 weeks, used the 13 weeks before them to pick blend weights and a bias correction, froze both, and scored once. The champion blends a pooled gradient boosting model with last year's same-week orders, adjusted for the planned promotion lift. I left out the planner forecast that ships with the dataset, because it was generated from the simulator's hidden demand signal.

Stage 2 turns a 30-week demand plan into projected inventory week by week, using on-hand stock, confirmed purchase orders, lead time, safety stock, target weeks of supply (WOS), minimum order quantity and pack size. Stage 3 flags exceptions at SKU, channel, region and week level, merges consecutive weekly flags into episodes, and ranks them with a fixed scoring formula. An LLM then drafts a short explanation for the top 20 episodes from structured facts only.

Findings

Observed
MeasureResult
Forecast error (WAPE), 13-week test window9.7% at 1 week ahead, 11.7% at 13 weeks
Naive last-week forecast, same rows18.1% at 1 week, 31.6% at 13 weeks
Projected fill rate, 30-week plan99.9% (267 shortage units against 232,324 units of demand)
Exceptions736 flagged weekly rows, merged into 225 episodes, 65 of them P1 or P2

WAPE is total absolute error divided by total actual orders.

Observed

The 99.9% fill rate hid one node. TV65-OLED-B in E-Commerce US-West missed 36% of demand in a Big Game promotion week, while the same SKU in Retail US-East held about 266 units above its target.

Observed

All 7 projected shortage weeks fell inside the SKU's replenishment lead time, so no new order could reach them. The orders the engine placed for those nodes landed after the event and showed up as excess.

Observed

Projected weeks of supply averaged 5.82 against a target of 5.21, because the order-up-to level added safety stock on top of target WOS.

Decisions tested

Proposed

I turned these findings into three decisions and measured each by re-running the same planning engine with the change applied. The results are simulated, not observed in operations.

DecisionEffect in the simulated 30-week plan
Move stock between nodes of the same SKU before expeditingShortage units 267 to 34. Fill rate 99.885% to 99.985%.
Count safety stock inside target WOS, not on top of itAverage inventory down $2.41M (12.5%). Fill rate unchanged in paired demand-stress runs.
Drop the lifecycle uplift from the demand plan30-week demand down 14,981 units. New purchase orders down $13.46M.
Uncertain

The uplift came from a lifecycle factor that read the holiday-quarter peak as growth. Whether real demand would have arrived at the higher level is unknown. If it had, weekly re-planning still filled 99.89% of units.

Assumption

The transfers assume one-week transit and ignore freight cost. The stress test varies demand but not supply timing. The 99.9% projection assumes every planned order arrives in full, although 4.0% of received purchase-order lines in the history arrived late.

Observed

Of the 12 LLM recommendations I reviewed, every number quoted matched the engine's output. 10 used only facts from their own packet. The other two referred to a linked shortage episode that was not in their packet, so the next fix is to include linked-episode facts in the packet.

Limits

Limit

The data is synthetic, and the simulator's own noise means no model should be expected to beat 8.4% WAPE, so accuracy here looks tighter than real demand would allow. Forecasts were validated only up to 13 weeks, on one test window. The 30-week plan uses a simpler rule-based demand blend that I did not backtest. The LLM text was drafted with ChatGPT and reviewed by hand, and the live API path is switched off.

The decision impacts are measured inside an engine and a dataset I built, so they show that the logic works, not what it would be worth to a real business. Next steps would be a rolling backtest over several windows and a year-over-year growth input to replace the lifecycle factor.

E-commerce promotion experiment: revenue impact and targeting
5,000 CUSTOMERS

A 20% coupon test where more people bought but revenue per customer did not rise. I reanalyzed the case data and worked out what a follow-up test would need.

Interactive dashboard →

Question

A 20% coupon was tested on about 5,000 customers. Did it raise revenue per customer, and is there any customer group worth testing again in the next campaign? I did not run the experiment. It is an educational case (Artea) that I reanalyzed, and the dashboard says so at the top.

Approach

I started by fixing the metric. Revenue per customer is purchase rate times transactions per purchaser times revenue per transaction, and every figure divides by all assigned customers, including people who bought nothing. I built that metric tree in SQL. Looking only at purchase rate would have made the coupon look like a clear win.

I then compared the two arms in Python, split the results by customer segment, and modeled profit under different margin, discount and cost assumptions. For the next campaign I worked out how large a follow-up test would need to be.

Findings

Observed
MetricCoupon vs. control
Purchase rate+1.70 percentage points
Revenue per transaction-19.8%
Revenue per customerNo increase. The confidence interval includes both a loss and a gain.

More people bought, but each purchase brought in about a fifth less, which cancelled the gain.

Uncertain

A lower revenue per transaction can come from the discount itself, from a different mix of buyers, or both. This data cannot separate them.

Observed

I compared 12 segments (acquisition channel, purchase history, cart status, weeks since last visit). None showed a revenue improvement after correcting for multiple comparisons.

Assumption

The profit scenarios depend on something the data does not say: whether recorded revenue is before or after the discount. For cart abandoners, the estimated profit changed from positive to negative on that single assumption. For the full customer base, the estimate was negative in every margin and cost combination I tried.

Recommendation

Proposed

I would not treat a broad coupon send as proven, and I would first confirm how discounts are recorded. Cart abandoners are a hypothesis for a new randomized test, not a validated target. A follow-up test sized to detect a gain of +1.00 per customer needs 30,692 customers. The next-campaign file lists 1,741 cart abandoners out of 6,000 candidates, so sample size is the main constraint on that test.

Limits

Limit

I picked cart abandoners after seeing the results, so the estimate for that group is biased upward and should not be used as a forecast.

I could not verify how customers were assigned to arms, so the balance and sample-ratio checks are diagnostics and not proof of randomization. The case data gives no currency, outcome window, or refund and cost data. The segment tests are exploratory because I had already looked at this dataset in coursework.

Citi Bike rebalancing analytics
132K TRIPS

Which stations lose or gain bikes, when, and whether the next hour's biggest net outflows can be predicted early enough to act on.

GitHub repository →

Question

Bike-share stations run out of bikes or fill up, and crews have to move bikes by hand. Which stations are affected, at what times, and can the stations that will lose the most bikes in the next hour be predicted early enough to act?

Data and approach

The data is about 132,000 Citi Bike trips from January 2018. I loaded them into SQLite and used SQL to count trips per station, work out net flow per station (starts minus ends), and look at demand by hour and weekday. I ran statistical tests on two comparisons: peak versus off-peak hours, and Subscribers versus Customers.

For prediction I trained a Random Forest to flag stations in the top 20% of net outflow in the next hour. I split the data by time instead of at random, so the model is always tested on a later period than it was trained on. Random splitting would let it see the future. I evaluated it with F1 score, a confusion matrix and feature importance. A Streamlit dashboard puts the map, demand trends and model output in one place.

Findings

Observed
QuestionResult
Where is demand?Trips concentrate at a small number of large hubs, such as Pershing Square North.
Which stations lose or gain bikes?Both net sources and net sinks exist. Net-outflow stations are the ones that need bikes sent to them.
When is demand highest?Afternoon and evening peaks, and busier mid-week days.
Do rider types differ?Subscribers take most of the trips and ride for less time. Customers take fewer, longer trips. Both differences held up in the tests.

These are summaries. The notebook in the repository has the numbers.

Limits

Limit

This is one winter month, so it does not show how patterns change with season or weather. The notebook also estimates how concentrated the imbalance is in the top five net-outflow stations. That is a measure of concentration and not a measured benefit from rebalancing, because I had no data on what crews actually did. The trip data is too large to include in the repository, so it has to be downloaded separately to rerun the notebook.

Pricing strategy evaluation under demand uncertainty
200 SIMULATIONS

How much to trust the price that looks best on a limited sample of customer demand.

Question

If you only have a limited sample of customer demand, how reliable is the price that looks best on that sample?

Approach

I compared four pricing methods across 200 simulations using 498 data points. I tested both the empirical and the heuristic models on data they had not been fit to, and compared how much revenue each alternative gave up.

Result

Observed

A conservative price did better out-of-sample than the naive optimum.

Proposed

I recommended the conservative price.
